Size — the same data has three legitimate numbers

  • 1,269 GB — raw uncompressed content, summing every column in the export. This is what the data actually is.
  • 949.6 GB — the live Postgres database (pg_database_size). Lower than raw because Postgres compresses large values, higher in other respects because it adds indexes, row overhead and dead space.
  • 170.4 GB — the public Parquet export, columnar and zstd-compressed (7.45× smaller than raw). 2,687 files.
  • So "compressed vs full" is not a choice between two copies of the truth. The Parquet holds the same rows as the ten exported tables; it is only stored more efficiently. What it does not carry is the indexes, the operational tables, and the dead space — none of which are content you would migrate.

Chains — three different numbers, all correct

  • 267 chains currently supported — supported: true on the live /chains endpoint. This is the one Kaan's summary calls "~265 EVM chains".
  • 420 chains configured — total entries in /chains, including 153 currently switched off.
  • 377 chains with at least one verified contract, ever — from their stats dataset.

Write load — and what one "write" actually is

  • Organic verifications: 34,639/day (7-day average), 37,545/day (30-day).
  • One verification is not one row. Measured across the corpus, each verified contract corresponds to 10.4 rows spread over the ten tables, and adds ~25.5 KiB to the database (from three size readings). So ~34k verifications/day is roughly 355k rows/day and ~850 MB/day.
    • 3.95 rows in compiled_contracts_signatures — the function/event selectors
    • 1.67 in sourcify_matches · 1.00 in verified_contracts · 1.00 in contract_deployments
    • 0.90 in code · 0.82 in compiled_contracts_sources · 0.56 in contracts
    • 0.13 in compiled_contracts and 0.14 in sources — the important ones. Roughly 87% of verifications add no new compilation and no new source file at all, because the contract is a copy of something already stored.
  • That dedup is the whole cost story: a verification of genuinely new code is expensive, a repeat is nearly free. It is also why blob storage scales with 5.7M compilations, not 43.8M contracts.
  • Bulk backfills are a different regime entirely: +2,774,661 contracts on 17 July alone, which their own issue attributes to a similarity-import job.
  • This is why any trailing average that spans July is misleading: the 90-day mean is 166k/day, the 30-day mean is 37.5k/day. The steady state is the 30-day figure.

Database growth

  • Three readings of pg_database_size: 939.9 GB (7 Aug), 942.3 GB (10 Aug), 949.6 GB (18 Aug).
  • That is +9.05 GiB in 11 days = ~24.7 GiB/month, or about 26.5 GB/month in decimal units. GiB and GB are not the same unit — a GiB is 7.4% bigger.
  • The widely quoted 189 GiB/month is 7.7× too high. At the observed 25.5 KiB per verification it would need ~259,000 verifications/day; the real figure is 33,878. It was measured across a window containing the July bulk import.

Traffic — unresolved, and it matters

  • Dec 2025 recap: "over 15 Million requests per day (all Sourcify APIs)", with "[4byte] serves over 7 Million requests per day".
  • May 2026 post: "[v2] is already serving 15M+ requests/day" — which reads as the contract API on its own.
  • Both are Sourcify's own words and they cannot both describe the same thing. The difference is a factor of two on any capacity target. Treated as a question, not a fact.

Their schema, and what actually fits in Arkiv

This is the question that decides the whole architecture, and it is now answered with measured per-column byte sizes rather than intuition. Live schema diagram: dbdiagram.io.

Why the diagram shows more tables than the export

  • The schema is the Verifier Alliance base — shared with Blockscout and Routescan — plus Sourcify's own additions.
  • 7 Verifier Alliance tables: code, contracts, contract_deployments, compiled_contracts, compiled_contracts_sources, sources, verified_contracts.
  • 3 Sourcify tables: sourcify_matches, signatures, compiled_contracts_signatures. Those ten are what gets exported.
  • The rest are operational and never exported: verification_jobs and verification_jobs_ephemeral (in-flight verification state), a signature-stats materialized view, and the migration bookkeeping table. Two more — session and sourcify_sync — were dropped in March and July 2026, so a diagram showing them is out of date.
  • The 75-byte similarity search is served by an expression index on code (substring(code FROM 1 FOR 75)), not by a separate table.
The number that decides the architecture

Of 1,269 GB of raw content, only 52.7 GB (4.1%) is small-row, index-shaped data. The other 1,216 GB (95.9%) is a handful of very large columns.

So the answer to "what goes in Arkiv and what goes elsewhere" is not a judgement call. Arkiv holds the 4% that is queryable identity and relationships. The 96% is content-addressed blobs that belong in storage addressed by hash, with only that hash in the entity.

So can we replicate their schema?

  • Not row-for-row, and we should not want to. Their schema is normalised for a relational engine with joins. Arkiv has no joins, so a faithful copy would need multiple round trips per lookup.
  • The shape that fits is a denormalised head entity. One Arkiv entity per verified contract, keyed by chainId:address, carrying the indexed attributes people actually filter on — chain, address, match type, compiler, compiler version, language, contract name, the code hashes, verification timestamp — plus hash references to the heavy artifacts.
  • At ~44M head entities and roughly 600-800 bytes each, that is ~30 GB of entity payload. The remaining 1,216 GB stays content-addressed, which is exactly how Sourcify already stores it — keyed by hash, deduplicated across chains.
  • The dedup is already in our favour: 43.78M verified contracts resolve to only 5,716,510 distinct compilations and 6,329,026 distinct source files. Blob storage scales with the small number, not the large one.
  • The open question is not "can it fit" but "what is the per-entity ceiling". We know a whole Arkiv transaction is capped at 128 KB. We do not have a documented per-entity payload limit, and sourcify_matches.metadata reaches ~186 KB in its heaviest block — past that cap on its own. That is an engineering question, not a Sourcify one.

The candidate slice

  • 4byte signatures is the strongest first surface: 9,920,797 rows, 85 bytes each, 0.84 GB raw — and it carries a large share of their traffic. It is self-contained, has no blob problem at all, and is a pure lookup: the shape Arkiv serves best.
  • But it proves serving, not migration. And their search endpoint supports wildcard semantics that simple prefix matching does not reproduce, so we offer exact lookup only and claim nothing about search until it is proven.

My own context

What changed, what we got wrong, and the vocabulary — so nothing here surprises you in a meeting.

Glossary of the terms that keep coming up

  • GiB vs GB. GiB is binary (1,073,741,824 bytes), GB is decimal (1,000,000,000). A GiB is 7.4% larger. Sourcify's cost issue quotes GiB; their export stats are raw bytes. Our organic growth figure is 24.7 GiB/month = 26.5 GB/month — same thing, two units.
  • Raw vs Postgres vs Parquet. Three sizes for one dataset: 1,269 GB raw content, 949.6 GB on disk in Postgres (adds indexes and dead space, compresses large values), and 170.4 GB as zstd Parquet. None is wrong; they measure different things.
  • Export rows vs live rows. The Parquet export is append-only: a full file is never rewritten, so updated rows are appended and the old version stays. That is why sourcify_matches shows 74.2M rows in the export but 42.7M live — about 31.5M superseded versions, 42.4% of the export. Practical consequence: the export cannot be replayed as a backfill without deduplicating by primary key and keeping the newest.
  • Index-shaped vs blob-shaped. Our own coinage, but measured: 4.1% of the bytes are small rows you filter on; 95.9% are a handful of large text and JSON columns.

Corrections we made to our own earlier work

  • Chains. We published "497 configured". It is 420 configured, 267 supported, and 377 with at least one verified contract. Three metrics that were being used interchangeably.
  • 4byte signatures. We published 9,340,765 from their live stats endpoint, which reports its own refreshed_at of 6 July. Exact today: 9,920,797.
  • "We lost the migration argument." Wrong and self-defeating: issue #2866 compared hosted Postgres providers and never evaluated Arkiv.
  • Growth causality. We attributed the 4× to Foundry and Hardhat defaults. Their recap states the defaults but attributes nothing. Removed — and turned into a question for Kaan.
  • Our daily curve. Accurate for rates (+0.1% against Kaan's 7 Aug figure) but drifts on historical totals (+12.7% at end-2025). Quote rates from us, absolute history from them.
  • Our own load claims. Three overstatements, corrected in the Engineering tab.
