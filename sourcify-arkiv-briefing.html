Start here — the three-minute version

Sourcify is much bigger than any of our documents said: 43.78 million verified contracts, not the ~11M we had on file. It grew roughly 4× in under eight months. Their database is 949.6 GB and growing at about 25 GiB/month organically — not the 189 GiB/month figure that has been circulating, which came from a window containing a one-time bulk import.

The strategically important finding is that we can already build a representative migration corpus from public data alone. What we genuinely cannot see is how Sourcify is used — the request mix, the peaks, what gets updated versus written once. That is the entire content of the message to Kaan.

The one-line strategy

We are not proposing another lift-and-shift to a different hosted Postgres — they evaluated that in July and correctly said no. We are asking whether a decentralized, queryable serving and ownership layer changes the model. September should be a measurable shadow test with no production cutover, sized to produce the evidence a real migration would need.

On that last row, precision matters: 256.7 GB is simply pg_database_size minus the ten tables. It is a physical footprint not explained by the exported relations — which could be other tables, materialized views, system catalogs, or bloat. We cannot call it "unexported data" without a relation inventory from them.

The finding that changes how a migration would work

The Parquet export is append-only: once a file is full it is never rewritten. So when a row is updated or replaced, the export keeps both versions. Measured today:

sourcify_matches holds 74,196,213 rows in the export against 42,733,948 that Sourcify measured live on 10 August — roughly 31.5 million superseded rows, 42.4% of the export. compiled_contracts shows the same pattern at 9.7%.

Consequence: the export cannot be replayed as a backfill. Anything reading it must deduplicate by primary key and keep the latest version, or we would write tens of millions of dead entities. It also means every "row count" taken from the export is an upper bound on live data, not a measurement of it.

What we got wrong, and what is now corrected

independent review + our own re-measurement

A second technical review challenged the first version of this document. Most of it held up; several items did not. Everything below has been re-measured rather than argued.

C1 Chain counts were wrong. Three different metrics were being used interchangeably. corrected

We published "497 configured". The live /chains endpoint returns 420 entries, of which 267 have supported: true. Separately, 377 chains appear in their historical stats dataset with at least one verified contract.

All three are real and none is a substitute for the others. Kaan's own summary says "~265 EVM chains", which matches the 267 supported figure.

C2 The 4byte signature count we published was a stale counter. corrected

We quoted 9,340,765 from the live /stats endpoint. That endpoint reports its own refreshed_at: 2026-07-06 — the counter is six weeks old.

Exact count from the Parquet footers today: 9,920,797. The public counter understates by 580,032 (5.8%). Worth telling Kaan, since their own dashboard is the thing that is stale.

C3 "We lost the migration argument" was wrong and it weakened our position. corrected

Sourcify's July cost review compared conventional hosted Postgres providers — Neon, Supabase, Crunchy, Aiven, Hetzner, Scaleway. It never evaluated Arkiv. Saying we lost concedes an argument that was never had.

Correct framing: we are not proposing another hosted-Postgres lift-and-shift; we are testing whether a decentralized queryable serving and ownership layer changes the technical and economic model. Their review is still useful — it tells us the bar — but it is not a verdict on us.

C4 We attributed growth to Foundry and Hardhat without a source. removed

Their mid-2026 recap does say Foundry and Hardhat now verify to both Sourcify and Etherscan by default. It does not attribute the contract growth to that. We stated the causality anyway.

What is safely sourced: 11M at end-2025, 38.5M on ~1 July 2026, 43.78M today. State the numbers, let Kaan supply the cause — it is a good question to ask him rather than a claim to make at him.

C5 Our own daily curve is reliable for rates, not for historical absolute levels. qualified

Validated against their published figures: our curve gives 43,408,733 on 2026-08-07 against Kaan's 43,367,321 — +0.1%. But at 2025-12-31 it gives 12.40M against their stated 11M (+12.7%), and 39.56M on 1 July against 38.5M (+2.7%).

The divergence comes from how we forward-fill chains that report sparsely. Day-over-day deltas are unaffected, so the write-rate figures stand. Historical totals should be quoted from Sourcify, not from us.

C6 The "15M requests/day" figure has two conflicting official scopes. open question

Their December 2025 recap: "Our APIs serve over 15 Million requests per day (all Sourcify APIs)" plus "[4byte] serves over 7 Million requests per day".

Their May 2026 post on retiring API v1: "[v2] is already serving 15M+ requests/day" — which reads as the contract API alone.

Both are Sourcify's own words, eight months apart, and they cannot both describe the same thing. The difference is roughly a factor of two on any capacity target. This is now a question, not a claim.

C7 We overstated our own load evidence. Corrected in the engineering section. corrected

Three problems with how we presented the Arkiv test: we compared verifications/day against entities/second as if they were the same unit; we reported "zero failures" while omitting that a large share of submissions were turned away under queue pressure; and we treated a public gateway quota as if it measured the read engine's capacity.

All three are fixed in the engineering section, which now separates what is measured from what is assumed. This matters more for our credibility than for the plan.

C8 Held up: the growth-rate correction, the append-only export finding, and the CDN point. confirmed

Growth. The 189 GiB/month figure comes from a July issue and was measured across a window containing a bulk import — our curve shows +2,774,661 contracts on 2026-07-17 alone, and the issue itself attributes the spike to their similarity-import job. Three readings of pg_database_size across 11 days give ~24.7 GiB/month. Sustaining 189 would need about 259,000 verifications/day against an observed 33,878.

Append-only export. Now confirmed with exact counts, above.

CDN. Their CDN is switched off while 88.6% of successful GETs are repeat URLs, costing roughly $700/month. Any serving proposal has to acknowledge that turning it on is their cheapest option — though it buys none of decentralized ownership, independent replication, or queryability.

Tomorrow — Arkiv engineering

Internal only

Framing: we are not here to show numbers about our own network. It is a testnet under construction and any figure we quote today will be stale by September. We are here to (a) correct three claims we made that our own evidence does not support, and (b) leave with owners for six questions that gate everything we can promise Sourcify.

First: three corrections to our own load story

What we saidWhat the evidence actually supports
"Their write load is 0.4 entities/s against our 107/s — solved." Unit mismatch. ~34k verifications/day is not ~34k entities/day: one verification fans out across several relations, and any Arkiv mapping produces multiple entities per contract. The honest statement is that the headroom looks large and is unproven until replayed with real Sourcify-shaped records.
"107 entities/s sustained, zero failures." Incomplete in our favour. Zero failures among included writes, while a large share of submissions were turned away under queue pressure. We must report attempted / admitted / included / rejected separately, or the number is not honest.
"The read gap is 5,800×." Category error. That compares Sourcify's workload to a public gateway quota, which is a policy, not a measurement of the read engine. The real position: no read-capacity benchmark exists, and the public quota makes it untestable. That is a missing benchmark and an access problem, not a proven 5,800× deficit.

Then: six questions, each with an owner

  1. Read path and read benchmark. What read access exists for a named design partner on the September public testnet, and can we get a concurrent-read benchmark rather than periodic samples? Everything else is downstream of this. blocker · owner: whoever owns API keys and metering
  2. Query capability, stated conservatively. For each of prefix matching, inequality, ordering, projection, cursor stability and page limits: is it exposed in the SDK, implemented, verified on the deployed build, or planned? We have been treating a feature matrix as if it were a benchmark. owner: core protocol
  3. Does prefix search actually fit the similarity use case? Sourcify searches by the first 75 bytes of runtime bytecode. Prefix matching existing in the API is not proof it fits the attribute size and type constraints, or that it performs at tens of millions of entities. owner: core protocol · needs a test, not an opinion
  4. Ownership. Contract-account or multisig ownership that can still update, delete and extend. An EF-funded non-profit cannot put a public good behind a single private key. This is a governance blocker, not a preference. owner: core protocol / product
  5. Durability guarantees, not API semantics. Permanent expiry now exists in the API. Separately we need disk retention, snapshot and restore, full-sync reconstruction, and behaviour across network resets and releases. owner: infra
  6. Payload ceiling and fan-out. Hard per-entity payload limit and per-transaction input limit. Their compilations average tens of KB and some source bundles are far larger. owner: core protocol
The ask that unlocks September

One named technical owner on our side for the Sourcify POC, and a commitment to a staged benchmark: 100k representative entities first, then 1M, before anyone quotes a number for the full 43.8M corpus. Extrapolating a short synthetic test to a multi-day migration is exactly the mistake we just had to correct.

The two-track proposal

A single 4byte pilot proves a serving path but says nothing about whether a migration works. Two narrow tracks answer both, and both are sized so failure is cheap.

TrackWhat it isWhat it provesGate to pass
Functional 100k current verification heads, stratified by chain, match type, compiler and payload size, plus a 10k subset carried losslessly with its full dependency closure. Adapter speaks their API shape; answers compared automatically against theirs. That the data model survives contact with real records, not that it is fast. 100% hash parity on the lossless subset; idempotent re-import; resume from cursor after a crash
Capacity One hot read route chosen by Sourcify. 4byte exact lookup is our candidate; they may prefer another. Replay against their real route mix once they share it. Whether the read path can carry a real workload at all. replay at 2× their measured peak; latency and error thresholds set by them, not by us

Carrying a slice losslessly means the full dependency closure travels with each record, so the test exercises a real migration rather than a pointer. On 4byte specifically: it is an attractive first surface — 54.7 GB, 5.8% of their database, carrying a large share of their traffic. But their search endpoint supports wildcard semantics that simple prefix matching does not reproduce, so we should offer exact lookup only and make no claim about search until it is proven.
